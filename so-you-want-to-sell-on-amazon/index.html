So You Want to Sell on Amazon

Gary Burns December 19, 2025

  • Key Takeaways
    Amazon is not a flexible storefront — it’s a rigid, rule-based system where many decisions are permanent and mistakes compound over time.
  • Testing and “figuring it out as you go” can backfire on Amazon. Even deleted products can leave behind conflicts that are difficult to undo.
  • GTINs (Global Trade Item Numbers) identify real, physical products and are permanent once used. SKUs are internal tools — confusing the two is a common and costly mistake.
  • Product structure must be planned before anything is created. Parents must exist before children, and incorrect variation trees are one of the biggest pitfalls for new sellers.
  • Amazon prioritizes compliance over creativity. White-background primary images, structured copy, and strict formatting rules are not optional.
  • Changes on Amazon are slow and inconsistent. Edits may take hours or days to appear, and repeated changes can make problems worse, not better.
  • Details like product weight, dimensions, and packaging are critical backend data points — not minor fields — and incorrect values can affect eligibility, fees, and stability.
  • Amazon rewards patience, structure, and precision. Sellers who rush or assume flexibility often end up rebuilding their listings from scratch.

Taming the mighty Amazon is not easy. It’s one of the most powerful e-commerce platforms on the planet, but it’s also one of the most rigid, unforgiving, and unintuitive systems you’ll ever work with. Amazon does not encourage experimentation, it does not reward improvisation, and it rarely explains why something isn’t working.

If you’re thinking about selling on Amazon, this article is meant to give you a realistic picture of what’s involved — not the polished success stories, but the actual process. Amazon can absolutely work for your business, but only if you approach it with structure, patience, and a clear understanding of its limitations.

Step 1: Understand That Amazon Is a System, Not a Storefront

Most people come to Amazon expecting it to behave like Shopify or WooCommerce — flexible, visual, and forgiving. Amazon is none of those things. It operates more like a massive rule-based system layered on top of aging infrastructure. Some parts of the interface look modern, but behind the scenes, many tools are antiquated, slow, and surprisingly fragile.

Edits don’t always save. Pages crash. Fields appear editable but aren’t. Changes can take hours — sometimes days — to appear across Amazon’s systems. And when something goes wrong, the platform rarely tells you what the problem is, only that there is one.

One of the clearest examples of this came early on when we set up a series of test products to understand how Amazon handled variations and identifiers. Everything looked correct on the surface, but once the products were submitted, we discovered that Amazon had permanently tied our paid GTINS to these test products — even after they were deleted. What was meant to be a harmless test ended up creating conflicts that had to be carefully untangled later.

That experience taught a critical lesson: Amazon remembers everything, and you don’t get unlimited do-overs.

Step 2: Learn the Language Early (GTINs and SKUs Matter More Than You Think)

Two acronyms show up everywhere on Amazon, and misunderstanding them causes more problems than almost anything else: GTIN and SKU.

A GTIN stands for Global Trade Item Number. It’s the barcode number that identifies a specific physical product anywhere in the world. Amazon checks GTINs against the official GS1 database to confirm that:

  • The product exists
  • The brand matches exactly
  • The GTIN hasn’t been reused improperly

Amazon treats GTINs as permanent. Once a GTIN is used, Amazon associates it with an internal ASIN (their product ID), and that relationship doesn’t disappear just because you delete a listing. This is where many sellers get stuck — especially if they experiment, reuse GTINs, or try to “clean things up later.”

A SKU, on the other hand, is your internal identifier. It’s how you track products inside Seller Central. SKUs are flexible and can be changed or replaced, but GTINs are not. Confusing the two leads sellers to believe they have more freedom than they actually do.

The key takeaway here is simple:
GTINs identify the physical product. SKUs organize it internally. One is permanent, the other is not.

Step 3: Plan the Entire Product Structure Before Creating Anything

This is one of the biggest pitfalls for new Amazon sellers: jumping straight into creating products without designing the structure first.

Amazon uses a parent–child product model, and it is extremely strict about it. Parent products are non-buyable placeholders used only to group variations. Child products are the actual sellable items. The order matters — parents must be created before children — and once a structure is in place, many elements cannot be changed.

This is where things often go wrong:

  • Products are created in the wrong order
  • Variations are grouped incorrectly
  • Parents are treated like real products
  • Sellers assume they can “fix it later”

In reality, fixing it later often means deleting products, rebuilding structures, and dealing with lingering conflicts from Amazon’s memory of what existed before. This is exactly what happened with those early test products — the structure seemed harmless at the time, but it created long-term complications.

Amazon does not like flexibility. It likes clearly defined trees, consistent variation themes, and clean relationships between parents and children. If this step isn’t planned carefully, everything downstream becomes harder.

Step 4: Create Assets the Amazon Way (Not the Creative Way)

Amazon has very specific expectations when it comes to product assets, and creativity takes a back seat to compliance.

For primary images, Amazon expects:

  • A pure white background
  • A clear, front-facing product
  • No text, no badges, no overlays

Secondary images can tell more of a story, but even then, Amazon is strict about claims, consistency, and formatting. Uploading the wrong type of image can delay approvals or suppress listings.

Copy follows the same rules. Product titles, descriptions, and bullet points must be factual, structured, and compliant. Over-marketing, exaggerated claims, or vague language can trigger rejections or silent edits by Amazon’s system.

This is also where Amazon’s delays become very noticeable. You might upload images or edit copy and see nothing change for hours. In some cases, edits apply in one area but not another. Making repeated changes too quickly often makes things worse, not better.

Step 5: Final Details Are Not “Small Details” on Amazon

On most platforms, things like product weight, dimensions, and packaging details feel minor. On Amazon, they are not.

These fields affect:

  • Shipping calculations
  • Storage fees
  • Product eligibility
  • Backend validations

Some of these details can only be reliably edited through inventory files, not the user interface. Others appear editable but get overwritten automatically. Incorrect weights or units can cause listings to behave unpredictably or fail internal checks.

This is one of the last steps, but it’s also one of the most important. Once these values are locked in and accepted, correcting them later can be frustrating and time-consuming.
